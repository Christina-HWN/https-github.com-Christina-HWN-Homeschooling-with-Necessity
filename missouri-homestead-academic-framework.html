01 • Framework

Demystifying the hour-and-documentation approach

This page is designed as an educational planning framework, not a legal determination. Use the documentation structure to make learning visible, then compare it against the current Missouri requirements and the student's actual educational plan.

Core academics

Document reading, writing, mathematics, science, social studies, and other academic work embedded in projects rather than treating every subject as a separate worksheet.

Home & farm learning

Capture practical work such as animal care, food preservation, gardening, construction, maintenance, water systems, and household management as documented learning experiences.

Electives & projects

Use deeper projects, research, art, technology, entrepreneurship, environmental studies, and other interests to build a strong elective record and portfolio.

Language Arts

Use the homestead as a reason to read, research, write, communicate, and think critically.

Skills
Reading • vocabulary • grammar • composition • research • communication
Evidence
Research notes • journals • reports • instructions • presentations • reflections
Big question
Can I find reliable information, understand it, and communicate what I learned?

Laid-Out Example • Poultry Nutrition Research

  1. Start with a real problem: The flock's feed needs to be evaluated. Ask, “What does a healthy chicken diet require, and how do I know a source is trustworthy?”
  2. Research: Find 3–4 credible sources. Record the author/organization, date, purpose, and key claims. Compare where the sources agree or disagree.
  3. Vocabulary: Define terms such as protein, carbohydrate, amino acid, calcium, nutrient, deficiency, ration, and forage in the student's own words.
  4. Note-taking: Create a two-column research page: What the source says / What I think it means for our flock.
  5. Writing: Produce a 1–2 page Poultry Care & Feeding Guide with headings, organized paragraphs, cited sources, and specific recommendations.
  6. Final reflection: Write a short response explaining what changed in the student's understanding, which source was most useful, and what question remains.

Mathematics

Make quantities, measurement, patterns, data, money, and mathematical reasoning part of the work.

Skills
Arithmetic • algebra • geometry • statistics • ratios • financial math
Evidence
Measurements • formulas • tables • graphs • budgets • calculations
Big question
Can I use mathematics to make a real decision or solve a real problem?

Laid-Out Example • Design a Productive Garden

  1. Define the space: Measure the proposed garden and record length, width, paths, beds, and unusable areas.
  2. Geometry: Calculate area and perimeter for each bed. Draw the plan to scale and label dimensions.
  3. Ratios & unit conversion: Calculate plant spacing, convert inches to feet, and determine how many plants fit in each row.
  4. Materials: Calculate soil, compost, seed, mulch, or lumber quantities. Show the formulas instead of only recording the final number.
  5. Data: Track germination, plant growth, harvest weight, rainfall, or other chosen variables in a table and graph.
  6. Analysis: Compare expected and actual yield. Calculate percentages or averages and explain what the numbers suggest for the next planting.
Portfolio set: scaled garden plan • calculation sheet • data table • graph • yield analysis • written conclusion

Science

Use observation and investigation to understand living systems, matter, energy, Earth, and the environment.

Fields
Biology • chemistry • physics • Earth science • environmental science
Evidence
Observations • hypotheses • experiments • data • diagrams • conclusions
Big question
What can I observe, test, measure, and explain about the world around me?

Laid-Out Example • Compare Soil Conditions

  1. Ask: Does soil condition appear to affect plant growth?
  2. Plan: Choose two or more soil conditions to compare and identify the variable being changed and the measurements being collected.
  3. Background research: Read about soil texture, organic matter, drainage, pH, nutrients, and plant requirements.
  4. Collect data: Record observations and measurements consistently. Photograph the setup and keep dated notes.
  5. Analyze: Organize results in a table or graph. Look for patterns and consider other variables that could affect the outcome.
  6. Conclude: Write a conclusion that answers the original question and explains whether the evidence supports the student's interpretation.
Portfolio set: research notes • investigation plan • dated observations • data table • graph • photos/diagram • conclusion

Social Studies

Connect the homestead to people, places, systems, history, government, geography, and economic decisions.

Fields
History • civics • geography • economics • culture • government
Evidence
Maps • timelines • source notes • essays • interviews • economic analysis
Big question
How do people, places, policies, history, and economic systems shape the food and communities around us?

Laid-Out Example • Investigate Local Agriculture

  1. Geography: Map major agricultural areas and identify how land, water, climate, and transportation influence what is produced.
  2. History: Build a timeline showing how farming, land use, or a local agricultural industry changed over time.
  3. Primary sources: Interview a farmer, producer, extension professional, or community member and record the questions and answers with permission.
  4. Economics: Compare production costs, consumer prices, supply and demand, or the economics of buying locally.
  5. Civics: Research one current rule, policy, or public decision affecting agriculture and identify who makes the decision and how citizens can participate.
  6. Argument: Write an evidence-based position using multiple sources and explain why the evidence supports the conclusion.
Portfolio set: annotated map • historical timeline • interview notes • source log • economic comparison • policy notes • evidence-based essay

Integrated Academic Example

One chicken project can become an entire academic investigation.

A student might research poultry nutrition, calculate feed costs, compare feed labels, observe the flock, graph consumption, study animal biology, write a care guide, and present a recommendation. The point is not to claim that “taking care of chickens” automatically equals a course. The point is to identify the actual academic work the student performed and preserve evidence of it.

Three things to keep separate

1. Missouri requirements: the legal framework that applies to the student's homeschool. 2. Academic mapping: the subject and skill connections supported by the actual learning. 3. HWN documentation: a practical way to preserve evidence of that learning. This framework helps organize the last two; it does not replace the first.
